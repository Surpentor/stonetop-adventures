Entry 1: The Return of Ivan and the Shadow of the Hdor

First of Spring, Year 150

Recorded by Judge Wapner Sati within the chaotic halls of The Chronicle, on the night of the First of Spring, Year 150 of the Founding.

The day began with unease. Word spread through Stonetop that Ivan had returned from the east — alone, weary, yet unbroken. The villagers gathered at the edge of town, suspicion heavy in their eyes. Mirgan and Aedre approached first, cautious but curious, while I followed close behind. Aedre searched him for signs of deceit or corruption; he was clean.

We gathered at the pavilion. The air was thick with tension and the scent of poor ale. A watchman struck Ivan on the way, and I demanded reason — the man answered that Ivan had led the raid against this village. True enough, and so he received only a warning. Ivan, calm despite the insult, asked for water; Mirgan pressed the Milange on him instead, bitter Malort and orange, offered in good faith by the good people of Stonetop. He drank it down, and gagged, and talked.

Then came his tale. He spoke of The Hillfolk, ancient bands whose blood feuds ran back before his own birth — feuds that have now ceased. A stranger has appeared, a man come up out of somewhere, whom The Ash Herd name only by a whispered word: Hdor. He unites the tribes through fear and mystery. Even Yannic of The Wolf Fang, proud and defiant, who refused the alliance loudly before several tribes, was taken and returned two days later, kneeling. His people folded in behind him, and the Steplands gather as one beneath the Hdor's shadow.

Ivan's words chilled the pavilion. He spoke of a host a thousand strong, four hundred of them trained soldiers, gathering nearer to Stonetop than to any other place, and bound, he believes, for The Ruined Tower and whatever lies in the tunnels beneath it. I pressed him for the truth, my hammer beneath his chin, and found no lie in him. He came back because he is sorry for what he did, and because these people are in our territory. Rory, ever the zealot, invoked rites that vexed me, yet revealed no falsehood — Ivan sat calm and content in Helior's light, and whatever is in the man, it did not flinch from it.

I dispatched messenger ravens to every village and city within reach, bearing Ivan's warning. Then I turned to the Chronicle — that interdimensional archive of chaos — to seek the truth behind the names he uttered: Hdor, the Ruined Tower, crinwin, and Yannic.

Within its shifting shelves I found a tome titled Strange Migrations of the Hill Folk. Its pages spoke of Spirit Talkers — sorcerers bound by taboo, feared for their communion with unseen forces. A Hdor, it said, is one who breaks those sacred laws, wielding power for himself until it consumes him. Such beings summon spirits and beasts to attend and spy for them, and hoard arcana, or hunt for more. The Hillfolk drive them off but dare not slay them, for those who slay a Hdor are cursed.

Further reading revealed the Tower's origin: once the domain of the Tempest Lords, near-godlike Makers — perhaps the first of them — prideful charters of the seas and the stars. The Tower was the mast of their ship, catching wind and lightning, now grounded and hollow. Arcana of their making remain below, some said to be very powerful, in tunnels whose reach no one has mapped, where relic-seekers have braved the edges and not always returned.

And so the Chronicle grows — a record of rising storms and ancient pride. The Steplands march, the Tower waits, and the Hdor's name spreads like thunder across the plains.

Filed, insofar as anything here is filed, in Year 150 — a year the village has not yet named.

Entry 3: Chronicle of Curious Conveyances and Unlikely Diplomacies

The road to the Ruined Tower

Judge Wapner Satie Chronicle of Curious Conveyances and Unlikely Diplomacies. An Account of the Road to the Ruined Tower and the Hillfolk Conclave. Let it be known, dear reader, that few journeys begin with such innocent intentions and end with such bewildering revelations as the expedition undertaken by the stalwart company of Stovetop. I, Wapner Satie, have taken it upon myself to record our travels, for the roads they walked—crafted by the ancient Makers themselves—are seldom host to violence, yet often host to stories worth the telling. On the Road of Makers and Mispronunciations The party departed their humble town with supplies in hand and questions aplenty. Mirgan, a man of either mischief or mischief‑adjacent tendencies, continued to mispronounce Aedre's name with such consistency that one must assume it is deliberate. Aedre, in turn, filled the air with whistles so erratic and off‑key that even Picard's flute would blush. When asked to share something new, I confessed an enjoyment of Merlort, a beverage whose admirers are few, brave, and possibly cursed. Of Changing Seasons and Sinister Groves As we neared the crossroads, the air grew cold—unnaturally so—and the season itself seemed to shift beneath our boots. A strange light flickered overhead, neither sun nor star, and the nearby grove exuded a palpable aura of decay. Rori identified the dreaded Dool trees, and Aedre revealed their connection to the Pale Hunter, who despises them with legendary fervor. These trees, it is said, house dual spirits—never a comforting trait in flora. Mirgan wondered aloud whether cutting them down would be wise. No one answered. A wise choice, for when dealing with haunted timber, silence is often the safest reply. The Tale of Windy Gilroy The company recalled the ghost of Windy Gilroy, once Stovetop's village idiot, later rumored to be a sorcerer of immense power who lost control of his arcana and fled into the wilds. His spirit is said to haunt the roads, perhaps seeking the sanity he misplaced. Arrival at the Tower of Hillfolk Upon reaching the ruined tower—glorious in height though decrepit in form—we found it surrounded by zounds of hillfolk. The tower, rumored to house a Seed of Perpetual Prosperity deep within its catacombs, could feed Stovetop for generations. Yet one must always ask: if something can create endless bounty, what calamity might it also unleash? The hillfolk, strangely organized and peaceful, greeted us with nods of recognition. They had built a veritable city around the tower, and when asked who ruled them, the answer was Branick. Branick the Impossibly Sculpted Three warriors escorted the group to meet Branick, who proved to be an elderly man carved from the very concept of physical perfection. If oil were poured upon him, he would gleam like a divine idol. He carried the swagger of one who has visited death, punched it in the jaw, and returned. Mirgan attempted diplomacy with all the finesse of a man juggling eels. Rori stepped in with better questions, and soon Branick's attention turned to Mirgan's sacred spear—pun very much intended. A truth‑compelling enchantment was invoked, and Branick admitted his interest. Mirgan reluctantly surrendered the spear for examination. Branick recognized the arcana within and even claimed familiarity with the wood, boasting of his own "hardwood" with a pride that suggested both confidence and innuendo. Deals, Feasts, and Hillfolk Hospitality Branick vowed not to march on Stovetop and hinted at trade opportunities—no doubt influenced by the party's impressive grasp of negotiation, allegedly honed through Volo's Art of the Deal (a fine purchase, if I may say so). The night ended with feasting, singing, and camaraderie among the hillfolk as the party camped near the catacomb entrance. Branick, though soft‑spoken, proved himself a capable leader—stern when needed, generous when possible, and possessed of a physique that inspired both admiration and questionable commentary. Thus concludes this chapter of their journey. What lies beneath the tower, within the catacombs, and beyond the Seed of Prosperity remains to be seen. But rest assured, dear reader: I shall chronicle every curious twist, every perilous turn, and every mispronounced name. Take that Volo

Entry 4: Chronicle of the Hill Tower, Part II

The descent

Chronicle of the Hill Tower, Part II As recorded by Judge Wapner Satie, Morning broke over the Hill Tower with the reluctant glow of a sun that clearly wished it were shining somewhere else. Rori, ever the zealous herald of her storm‑kissed deity, was already awake—proselytizing to the hillfolk with the fervor of a street preacher and the subtlety of a brass band. A noble pursuit, yes, but one that tends to cling to the uneducated like burrs to a wool cloak. With lamp ignited, we descended the spiral staircase—an architectural nightmare that wound downward as though designed by a cartographer who hated joy. The darkness below was not merely absence of light, but a chasm of pure, unrepentant blackness. A place where weary adventurers go to die, or worse, continue adventuring. At last, a chamber greeted us. Light poured through high windows in dramatic beams, as if the gods themselves were wrestling for the privilege of illuminating our path. The room, however, bore the scars of previous explorers—ravaged, rummaged, and left in a state that suggested adventurers are, in fact, the true monsters of the world. Two paths awaited: west or east. We chose east. We always chose east. I don't know why. Tradition, perhaps. Or stubbornness. The Parlor of Cloud‑Ships and Bird Filth The Parlor was a gallery of ancient carvings—magnificent depictions of cloud‑borne ships hurling lightning upon cities below. A stirring vision of Tempest Lord grandeur… or it would have been, had the entire display not been defiled by the avian population of the tower. Birds, in their infinite disrespect, had claimed the room as their personal latrine. I hate birds. Mirgan, ever the scholar of stones, noted that the tower's rock matched that of Stovetop. He then launched into a tale of the Tempest Lords—Makers of floating castles and storm‑catching vessels. A history so grand it nearly made me forget the smell. Rori, seeking divine insight, urged us to watch for shards. Of what, she did not specify. Her god is fond of riddles. The Great Hall and the Stone‑Top Incident The Great Hall was a cathedral of disorder—dirty windows, scattered debris, and a stone table large enough to host a feast for giants. Hillfolk rummaged about like raccoons in a pantry. Rori pointed at the table and declared, "Hey look, stone top." No one laughed. I groaned, mostly because I didn't think of it first. One leg of the table was braced with extra stones, and upon a bronze plate Mirgan discovered runes—ancient sigils humming with thunderous potential. After he cleaned it, I deciphered the markings, confirming their storm‑touched nature. Mirgan became visibly excited. Embarrassingly excited. Lower‑region excited. Aedre, ever the shadow in the corner of one's eye, scouted two nearby doors. One led to a bathing chamber, the other a servant's passage. Both were unremarkable save for a lone broom—ominous in its stillness, clearly cursed, and wisely left behind. The Chamber of Foul Waters We entered a room filled with water that smelled as though it had been siphoned directly from the crypts of the dead. Massive pipes lined the walls, groaning with the threat of imminent explosion. We left quickly. Some horrors are not worth the XP. The Descent to the Forge Downward we continued, until a rhythmic bang… bang… bang… echoed through the stone. The unmistakable cadence of forging—though what was being forged, and by whom, was a question best left unanswered. At the bottom lay five tunnels and a hole descending into narrowing darkness. Rori lowered the lamp; the passage constricted unnaturally. Man‑made? Sorcery? Maker‑craft? Only descent would reveal the truth. One tunnel flickered with firelight. Aedre scouted and returned with news of constructs—mechanical beings, monotone and murderous. But stranger still, a massive green ghost drifted among them, sliming the machines with spectral ooze. I suggested "Slimer." It felt right. Mirgan, of course, was thrilled. Rori noticed and the two bickered like a couple ten years into a marriage neither remembers agreeing to. The Forge of the Tower We followed Mirgan into the forge. Heat slammed into us like the breath of the hells themselves. A colossal figure—thirty feet tall, hammer in hand—turned to face us. "WHO ARE YOU?" Its voice shook the chamber, rattling teeth and courage alike. We stated our intentions. Mirgan, in a display of conversational recklessness, engaged the creature directly. One wrong word and we would have been paste. The being declared its purpose: to restore the tower to its rightful glory. A noble, secular mission of reconstruction. It spoke of a mighty Storm Catcher, of demons from the hells who destroyed the tower, of the last Tempest Lord—a beautiful woman who built a town of faithful citizens. It recounted the fall of the Makers, the wars of gods, and the shattering of a great orb whose fragments lay hidden throughout the tower. Mirgan, naturally, provoked it. The creature attacked. Mirgran performed a maneuver that can only be described as "Troyish Achilles nonsense," contorting his body in ways that should be illegal, striking the creature while dodging its hammer. I, in mounting frustration, turned my justice upon the constructs. The battle was glorious, chaotic, and spiritually idiotic. Fire, as always, proved effective. Mirgan—now Migraine—had once again placed us in mortal peril. And I, tragically, was without Merlort.

Entry 5: The Chronicle of the Broken Tower, Part III

The aftermath

THE CHRONICLE OF THE BROKEN TOWER, PART III As set down by the wandering scribe of Judge Wapner Saite, in the year of smoke and sorrow.

Of the Ruins Beneath Fern Gully Adjacent And lo, the Tower groaned like an old god turning in its sleep. The constructs—those iron‑wrought abominations of forgotten craft—still littered the chamber, some twitching, some shattered, some glaring with the hateful glow of false life.

Migraine, ever the opportunist, sought loot among the debris. A Fival‑born instinct, some say—others call it madness. Yet while his fingers danced over broken gears, another doorway revealed itself, a mouth of shadow beckoning.

Judge Wapner Satie—hammer‑bearer, shield‑breaker, and wielder of "special access"—stepped through to his home‑realm of research and secrets. But fate, ever mischievous, allowed a construct to slither behind him, its gears whispering treachery.

He turned. He struck. The hammer fell like a verdict.

The construct stabbed him westward—why westward, none can say, save perhaps the gods of direction—but Wapner seized its head, kicked open the door, and hurled the metal serpent into its kin. The crash echoed like thunder in a cathedral.

Then, standing tall, blood warm upon his armor, he proclaimed:

"The judge is in. ALL RISE."

And rise they did—constructs without number, unleashing hell upon the party. Of Battle, Bedrolls, and Bedlam The thrown construct, insulted beyond measure, lunged again. Wapner met it with shield and fury, smashing it into scrap and clipping another besides. For a heartbeat he admired his stance—heroic, statuesque, undeniably majestic—until he realized he had StoveTop's ass pressed against him. History will debate the tactical relevance of this.

Aedre, daughter of the Steplands, leapt into the fray, blades singing, protecting Migraine with the ferocity of a storm‑born wolf. Meta‑learning occurred—some call it "oops we're still figuring out the rules"—but the battle raged nonetheless.

Aedre braced defensively. Wapner sprinted to reclaim his shield.

Unbeknownst to him—but knownst to all others—a construct prepared to Vlad‑the‑Impaler him from behind. Salvation came from an unlikely quarter: Rori, who distracted the creature by lowering their bedroll to their waist and performing what scholars now refer to as cannon‑firing thrusts. The construct, confused by this display of primal intimidation, redirected its murderous intent.

At last, battered and sparking, the constructs retreated. The party allowed them to flee, for victory had already been claimed. Wapner seized a CORE—glowing, humming, heavy with secrets—and they ascended from the basement. Of Branick, Lubed and Luminous At the exit stood Branick, ever slick, ever shimmering with his strange oils, light trickling down him like dew on marble. Migraine attempted negotiation, proving once more that he is a terrible negotiator. Rori spoke of the ghost's warning—that the Tower would have "removed" the hillfolk.

Branick demanded they leave. Wapner offered knowledge, secrets, truths of the Tower's inner workings. Branick remained unmoved.

Thus the party departed, swift as wind over dying embers.

Of Sorrow in Stovetop Stovetop lay wounded. Homes broken. Bodies torn. Smoke rising like mourning veils.

Aedre's father had fallen. Her grief was a storm no blade could parry.

Migraine sought his mother, who spoke of stolen grain—desperation gnawing at the edges of civilization.

Sullen, grim and weary, pulled Wapner aside. His words were heavy:

The attack was Crinwin work. Hunger drove them. Many were eviscerated. Many were desperate.

Thus ends this chapter of the Chronicle—written in ash, blood, and the trembling hope that the next dawn will bring answers rather than ruin.

Something in the tunnels beneath the tower, spoken of in the old stories: a seed, or a cache, that asks minimal tending. Nothing in the old stories is free. Where it lies, whether the Forgemaster knew of it, and what the tending costs are all open questions.

Threat

Ogmolok, the Buried Horror

What the Forgemaster remembered, under the Ruined Tower: when the Storm Lords' slaves rebelled and unleashed the things below, the forge-lord cities were buried. The tower endured as a bastion. Then the cultists of Ogmolok drove the defenders out, and Ogmolok came up from below. The Storm Catcher cast the spell that turned the things below to glass, and the same spell shattered her, broke the Maker's road for a stretch, and brought the tower down.

What the Dusk People told Brynna: Ogmolok lives. It is only contained, held by the Guardians and by those repairing the tower's defenses. Thistlewick warned that killing the Forgemaster could loose Ogmolok and his horde through the portal. The Forgemaster is already dead, by Mirgan's spear. Whether the containment still holds is the question the whole campaign now rests on.

The Gathered Bands

Seven Hillfolk tribes folded in under a Hdor, a thousand marching, four hundred soldiers, gathering nearer Stonetop than anywhere. Chief Yannic of the Wolf Fang refused publicly, was taken, and came back two days later kneeling. Ivan thinks they are after something in the Ruined Tower and its tunnels.

The Ruined Tower

Stormcatcher's Tower, bastion of the old wars, brought down by the Storm Catcher's own spell. The village knows the undertower has been delved and the Forgemaster slain there. What repairs to the tower's defenses continue, and who is doing them now, nobody in the village can say.
